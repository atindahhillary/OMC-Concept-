Tracking the data gaps behind Africa and Kenya's carbon markets

The One Mara Carbon Project publishes real removal, area, and livelihood figures for its Maasai Mara soil-carbon work — more than most projects on the continent disclose. This observatory uses that project as a benchmark to surface the data gaps still standing between Africa's carbon markets and a trustworthy, comparable, continuously verifiable market: fragmented registries, thin MRV capacity, opaque pricing, and inconsistent benefit-sharing disclosure.

The place behind the data

Why the Mara is worth this much care

Every gap tracked above is ultimately about this landscape and the people and wildlife who depend on it.

A grassland that runs on rain, rivers, and rotation

The Maasai Mara is the northern end of the wider Serengeti-Mara ecosystem, one continuous savanna straddling Kenya and Tanzania. Its grass is the whole system's fuel: it feeds the migration, it feeds Maasai cattle, and when it is grazed and rested in the right rhythm, its soil quietly pulls carbon down and holds it. Get the rotation wrong — too many cattle, too long, in one place — and the same grassland degrades, the soil carbon that took years to build releases, and wildlife corridors start to close. That single mechanism, grazing management, is the entire premise behind OMCP's carbon credits and behind most of the data gaps this observatory tracks: everything downstream depends on measuring this one process correctly.

Two million animals moving through one carbon ledger

Roughly 1.5 million wildebeest, alongside hundreds of thousands of zebra and gazelle, follow the rains in an annual circuit between the Serengeti and the Mara. That migration is also a slow-motion pressure test on the grassland OMCP is trying to protect: it grazes the same soils the project measures for carbon, and it depends on the same intact corridors between conservancies. A data gap in one is rarely isolated from the other — a wildlife corridor cut off by unplanned settlement or fencing shows up, eventually, as a soil carbon number that does not add up.

The people this market is supposed to pay

Maasai pastoralists have grazed these plains for generations under systems of shared, rotational land use that predate any carbon standard. Carbon finance only works here if it pays landowners and conservancies enough, visibly enough, to keep choosing rotational grazing over subdividing and fencing land for other uses. That is exactly why the benefit-sharing disclosure gap tracked above matters so much: without a public, comparable record of what communities actually receive, there is no way to tell whether carbon revenue is holding this system together or just extracting from it.

Biodiversity as the hardest number to fake

Elephants, lions, and topi need connected, healthy habitat, not just a favourable carbon balance on paper. That is why biodiversity and co-benefit claims are simultaneously the most emotionally compelling part of any carbon project's story and the hardest to verify from a spreadsheet. A restored grazing corridor either supports these populations or it doesn't — which is precisely the kind of ground-truth that the co-benefit reporting gap above says the market still cannot compare consistently across projects.

Methodology

How this registry is built

Each curated entry in the data gap registry is drawn from a named, publicly accessible report, news source, or peer-reviewed study, with a "last verified" date marking when the entry was checked against that source. Severity is a qualitative editorial judgement (critical / high / medium / low) based on how directly the gap undermines market integrity or community outcomes, not a standardised risk score. Status tracks whether the underlying gap is open, improving, partially resolved, or resolved as public information changes — Kenya's National Carbon Registry rollout is the clearest example of a gap actively moving from open toward resolved. Community signals are separate, unverified, and shown as submitted.
